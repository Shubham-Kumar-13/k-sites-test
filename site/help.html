Help & Documentation

Understanding K-Sites: A Comprehensive Guide

What is K-Sites?

K-Sites is a comprehensive Suite for Knock-out/down Studies that integrates multiple biological databases and literature mining to help you pick a safe gene to delete.

Multi-Database Evidence

QuickGO is always used; you can also select UniProt, NCBI Entrez, Alliance of Genome Resources, MyGene.info and Ensembl. More databases give a more complete pleiotropy score but take longer.

AI-Powered Predictions

RAG-based phenotype prediction from scientific literature.

Smart Filtering

Pleiotropy scoring to identify specific, safe targets.

Safety Assessment

Comprehensive knockout risk evaluation for each gene.

Methodology

Overview

K-Sites helps you pick a safe gene to delete. It integrates multiple biological databases and literature mining to rank the genes of a biological process by how specific their function is and how risky a knockout is likely to be.

The platform follows a multi-step pipeline from gene discovery to a safety recommendation for each candidate gene.

Step 1: Gene Discovery

The pipeline begins by querying the QuickGO database to retrieve all genes annotated with the selected Gene Ontology (GO) term in the specified organism. QuickGO is mandatory and is always queried.

You can additionally select other databases to broaden the search for biological-process annotations: UniProt, NCBI Entrez, Alliance of Genome Resources, MyGene.info and Ensembl. Selecting more databases usually raises the pleiotropy score and improves search quality, but each extra source adds query time.

Users can also filter by evidence type:

  • Experimental: Only experimentally validated annotations
  • Computational: Includes computational predictions
  • All: All available evidence types

This step typically returns 10-500 genes depending on the GO term specificity.

Step 2: Pleiotropy Scoring

Each discovered gene is scored for pleiotropy - the degree to which it participates in multiple biological processes.

The scoring algorithm:

  1. Queries all GO annotations for the gene
  2. Counts unique Biological Process terms
  3. Calculates exponential decay score

Genes with low pleiotropy scores are prioritized as they are more likely to produce specific, interpretable phenotypes upon knockout.

Step 3: Evidence Quality Assessment

GO annotations are evaluated based on their evidence codes:

  • Experimental (EXP, IDA, IMP, IGI, IEP): Direct experimental evidence
  • Phylogenetic (IBA, IBD, IKR, IRD): Evidence from related species
  • Computational (ISS, ISO, ISA, ISM, IGC, IBA, IBD): Predicted annotations
  • Author Statements (TAS, NAS): Non-traceable statements
  • Electronic (IEA): Automated predictions (lowest confidence)

Genes with higher proportions of experimental evidence receive better scores.

Step 4: Literature Mining

The literature score counts the PubMed articles that NCBI Gene links to the gene (the gene2pubmed list), not a text search of its name, so a gene symbol that is also an ordinary word is not confused with the gene. The count is shown with each gene and enters the composite score.

When enabled, the experimental RAG (Retrieval-Augmented Generation) predictor reads abstracts to guess knockout phenotypes, but only for genes that have neither a measured knockout result nor a lethality estimate.

Step 5: Measured Knockout Evidence

Every gene is looked up in the published knockout data (mouse, yeast, zebrafish, fly and worm knockouts, human cell-line screens, tolerance of loss-of-function variants, tissue expression and paralogs). See Measured knockout evidence. Genes above the pleiotropy cutoff are not dropped silently: they are listed as "not shown" together with their measured result.

Step 6: Safety Assessment

The final step turns the evidence into a verdict and advice, then ranks the genes:

  • The measured result for your knockout context decides the level (lethal: AVOID; reduced survival or lethal in some lines: HIGH_CONCERN; viable: SAFE, or CAUTION when the gene has many annotated roles, a shortened lifespan or infertility has been reported)
  • Where nothing was measured the verdict is never better than CAUTION and is marked "Not verified"
  • Specific advice follows from the finding: a conditional allele for a lethal gene, a double knockout where a close paralog may compensate, a tissue-specific knockout where expression is restricted

Genes are listed in this order:

  1. Safety level — SAFE → CAUTION → MODERATE_CONCERN → HIGH_CONCERN → AVOID.
  2. Evidence reliability — direct knockout/deletion → experimental essentiality → ortholog / distant hint → estimate → predicted / none.
  3. Composite score — higher-scoring genes within the same safety/evidence group come first.

Safety levels range from SAFE (safe to proceed) to AVOID (a complete knockout is lethal or kills the cells).

Output Generation

Results are provided in multiple formats:

  • HTML Report: Interactive, publication-ready summary
  • CSV Tables: Gene-level data for spreadsheets
  • JSON: Complete data for programmatic access

All results include detailed metadata, quality scores, and safety recommendations to support experimental design decisions.

Analytical Terms Explained

Understanding the scores and metrics used in K-Sites analysis reports.

Pleiotropy Score

How many biological processes a gene is annotated to (a hint about how broad its effects may be)

The Pleiotropy Score counts how many different biological processes a gene is annotated to. The more roles a gene has, the broader the effects of removing it may be.

How it's calculated:
  • Counts the Biological Process GO terms of the gene (with the evidence type you chose).
  • Score = 10 × (1 − e−0.3 × (n − 1)), where n is the total number of BP terms found for the gene (the n − 1 excludes the target GO term itself): 1 term gives 0, 2 terms 2.6, 3 terms 4.5, 5 terms 7.0, 8 terms 8.8, and 11 or more terms give 10.
Score Range:
0-2Very few annotated roles
2-5A few annotated roles
5-8Many annotated roles: expect broader phenotypes
8-10Very many annotated roles
Max pleiotropy (cutoff):

The score is always on a 0-10 scale. The max pleiotropy setting is a cutoff on that score: genes scoring above it are left out of the ranking (they are listed as "not shown" with their measured result). 0 keeps only the most specific genes, 10 keeps everything.

How far it can be trusted:

We compared the score with what is measured when the gene is knocked out in the mouse (13,733 genes, see the Validation page). Genes with a higher score do affect more organ systems and are more often lethal (14% lethal in the lowest band, 37% in the highest), but the link is modest: the rank correlation with the number of organ systems affected is 0.34, and the area under the ROC curve for telling lethal from viable is 0.61 (0.5 is a coin flip). Heavily studied genes simply have more annotations. Use the score as a hint about broad effects; the measured knockout result matters more.

Reference: GO Biological Process annotations; checked against MGI and IMPC knockouts (Validation page)

Specificity Score

The inverse of pleiotropy on a 0 to 1 scale - higher is more specific

The Specificity Score is 1 − pleiotropy_score / 10, so it runs from 0 (very many annotated roles) to 1 (a single annotated process). A pleiotropy score of 2.6 gives 0.74.

Interpretation:
0.8-1.0Few annotated roles
0.5-0.8A few annotated roles
0.2-0.5Many annotated roles
0-0.2Very many annotated roles
Usage:

It carries no information beyond the pleiotropy score; it is used because the composite score rewards genes with fewer annotated roles. Whether a knockout is safe is decided by the measured knockout evidence, not by this score.

Reference: Derived from the pleiotropy score

Composite Score

Weighted combination of specificity, evidence quality, literature support and conservation

The Composite Score combines four measures into one number between 0 and 1. It breaks ties between genes that have the same safety level and knockout-evidence tier. The safety level and evidence tier come from the measured knockout evidence, not from this score.

Formula:
Composite = 0.40 × Specificity + 0.25 × Evidence Quality + 0.20 × Literature + 0.15 × Conservation
Components (each from 0 to 1):
  • Specificity (40%): 1 − pleiotropy score / 10.
  • Evidence Quality (25%): the share of the gene's annotations that rest on experiments (weight 1.0), computational analysis (0.6) or electronic annotation (0.3).
  • Literature (20%): the PubMed articles that NCBI Gene links to the gene, on a log scale: 1 article or none gives 0, 10 give 0.33, 100 give 0.67, 1,000 or more give 1.
  • Conservation (15%): the share of five model species (human, mouse, rat, fly, worm) that have the gene or an ortholog of it (see Conservation).
Range:

0 to 1; higher means a better candidate by these measures. The weights are a design choice, not fitted to knockout outcomes.

Reference: Weighted scoring of annotation, literature and orthology data

Evidence Quality

Reliability of GO annotations based on evidence codes

The Evidence Quality score reflects the reliability of Gene Ontology annotations based on the types of evidence supporting them.

Evidence Code Hierarchy:
EXP Experimental Direct experimental evidence (highest quality)
IDA Direct Assay Direct experimental assay
IMP Mutant Phenotype Evidence from mutant studies
ISO Sequence Orthology Inferred from orthologs
IEA Electronic Annotation Computational prediction (lowest quality)
Score Calculation:

Weighted average based on the proportion of experimental vs computational evidence codes associated with the gene's GO annotations.

Reference: Gene Ontology Evidence Codes (https://geneontology.org/docs/guide-go-evidence-codes/)

Safety Level

Overall safety recommendation based on multiple risk factors

The Safety Level provides an overall assessment of the risk associated with knocking out a particular gene.

Safety Levels:
SAFE Safe to delete - standard knockout acceptable
CAUTION Generally safe - validation recommended
MODERATE_CONCERN Use with caution - heterozygous or validated knockout suggested
HIGH_CONCERN / AVOID High risk - conditional knockout, CRISPRi or another strategy preferred
What the level rests on:
  1. Measured knockout evidence comes first: whether the knockout organism or the cultured human cells survive (see Measured knockout evidence). A gene whose knockout is lethal is never rated safe, however specific it looks.
  2. The pleiotropy score adds caution to a viable gene that has many annotated roles (above 5: validate first), because its phenotypes will be broad.
  3. Without any measurement the verdict follows an estimate of lethality made from the measurements of the gene's human ortholog (cell lines, tolerance of loss-of-function variants, expression), and it is never better than CAUTION. The report says "Estimated from data". Where even that is impossible it says "Not verified".
How genes are ranked:

The result list is sorted by three layers, in this order:

  1. Safety level — SAFE, CAUTION, MODERATE_CONCERN, HIGH_CONCERN, AVOID.
  2. Evidence tier — see the table below.
  3. Composite score — used only as a tie-breaker within the same safety/evidence group.
Direct knockout/deletionA clean gene deletion with a viability call (highest reliability)
Experimental essentialityTraDIS/Tn-seq insertion screens or cell-line CRISPR screens; insertion tolerance is not the same as clean-deletion viability
Ortholog / distant hintKnockout result transferred from a related species
EstimateComputational prediction from human cell-line, population and expression data
Predicted / noneLiterature-based RAG prediction, or no usable data (lowest reliability)

Each gene card shows a badge such as "Direct knockout/deletion" or "Not verified" to say which evidence decided the verdict. Click the gene to see the detailed source records.

Reference: K-Sites integrated safety assessment algorithm

Measured knockout evidence

What has actually been measured when the gene is lost

K-Sites does not guess how harmful a knockout is where somebody has already measured it. For each gene it looks up:

  • Whole-organism knockout. Mouse: the literature curated by MGI (knockout alleles in homozygous mice) and the systematic IMPC viability screen. Human and rat genes use the knockout of their one-to-one mouse ortholog (Alliance of Genome Resources). Yeast: SGD deletion phenotypes. Zebrafish: ZFIN mutant phenotypes (clean homozygous mutants only). Fly: FlyBase allele lethality phenotypes. Worm: WormBase allele and RNAi lethal / sterile phenotypes. For the genes of zebrafish, fly and worm that these databases do not cover, and for Xenopus, the mouse ortholog's knockout is shown as a hint only. Plants and other organisms have no measured data. The result is lethal (with the stage of death), subviable (fewer homozygotes than expected) or viable, with the number of knockout lines behind it and links to the papers.
  • Human cell lines. The percentage of 1,178 cancer cell lines in which a CRISPR knockout kills the cells (DepMap 24Q4). Essential: most lines or DepMap's common essentials; sometimes essential: 10-50%; rarely essential: under 10%.
  • Tolerance in people (gnomAD LOEUF), tissue expression (GTEx; a tissue-restricted gene can be removed in those tissues only) and close paralogs (Ensembl; they can take over the gene's work and hide the phenotype).
How to read the label on each gene:
Direct knockout/deletionthe gene was knocked out or deleted in the organism
Experimental essentiality (not a clean knockout)a TraDIS/Tn-seq insertion screen or a cultured-cell CRISPR screen measured essentiality; insertion tolerance does not prove clean-deletion viability
Measured in the mouse orthologthe mouse gene was tested; the result is carried over to the human or rat gene
Estimated from data (not measured)no knockout was measured; the chance that it is lethal in mice is estimated from the human ortholog's cell-line, population and expression data (a model tested on genes with known outcomes: see the Validation page)
Predicted from the literaturenothing was measured and no estimate exists; a keyword model read abstracts (experimental, see below)
Not verifiedno measurement and no prediction: check MGI, IMPC or DepMap yourself
Things to know:
  • When knockout lines disagree (for example a classical null is lethal but a different allele or strain is viable) the report says so and rates the gene at least HIGH_CONCERN if any line was lethal.
  • "Viable" does not mean "unaffected": the organ systems with a reported phenotype are listed, and a shortened lifespan or infertility lowers the rating to CAUTION.
  • A mouse-lethal gene can be fine in cultured cells (it is needed for development, not in every cell), so both results are always shown.
  • The data come from the files published with this site, refreshed monthly. A "Data sources" list with the version is part of every report.
Reference: MGI, IMPC, Alliance of Genome Resources, SGD, ZFIN, FlyBase, WormBase, DepMap 24Q4, gnomAD v4.1, GTEx v8, Ensembl Compara; checks on the Validation page

What will you knock out?

Cultured cells or a whole organism: it decides which measurement the verdict follows

The same gene can be harmless to knock out in a cell line and impossible to knock out in an animal. TDG and POLB are good examples: mouse embryos lacking them die, but human cell lines tolerate their loss.

  • Cultured cells (the default for human): the verdict follows the DepMap CRISPR screens. The mouse result is still shown, as a reminder that an animal model would need a conditional allele.
  • Whole organism (the default for mouse, rat, yeast, zebrafish, fly, worm and everything else): the verdict follows the knockout organism (or the mouse ortholog).

Both measurements are shown for every gene whichever you choose; the card marks the one that decides the verdict.

Reference: DepMap (cell lines); MGI / IMPC / SGD / ZFIN / FlyBase / WormBase (organisms)

RAG Phenotype Prediction (experimental)

A literature-based guess for genes with no measured knockout data

Retrieval-Augmented Generation (RAG) phenotype prediction reads PubMed abstracts and guesses the likely knockout phenotype for genes that have no measured result and no cross-species estimate. It is switched off by default.

When to use it:
  • Turn it on for short lists (a few dozen genes), well-studied organisms, and when you can wait a few minutes. It gives at least a literature hint for genes that otherwise have no data.
  • Leave it off for human and mouse (measured data already cover most genes), for organisms like zebrafish, Xenopus, fly, worm or rat where thousands of genes have no data, for lists above ~100 genes, and when you want the result quickly.
Optional hosted language-model API key:

You can paste an API key for one of the supported providers to let a larger language model read the abstracts. The key is used only in your browser and is never sent to the K-Sites server. If the key is blank or the API fails, the analysis falls back automatically to the free browser model (MiniLM + keyword patterns).

Supported providers:

  • Kimi (Moonshot) — default; strong context understanding.
  • OpenAI — gpt-4o-mini or other chat models.
  • Anthropic — Claude 3.5 Sonnet via the Messages API.
  • Qwen (Alibaba) — DashScope compatible endpoint.
  • DeepSeek — deepseek-chat.
  • Grok (xAI) — grok-2-latest.

Why it helps: The default browser model matches keywords and counts how often words like “lethal” or “viable” appear. It works for clear cases but can be misled by negation (for example, “the knockout was not lethal”), by sentences about a different gene in the same paper, or by conflicting reports. A hosted language model reads the abstracts as a whole, understands context, resolves contradictions, and usually returns a more reliable severity call.

How to get a key:

  1. Choose a provider in the “Language model provider” drop-down on the analysis page.
  2. Create an account with that provider and generate an API key:
    • Kimi: platform.moonshot.ai → API keys (starts with sk-).
    • OpenAI: platform.openai.com/api-keys (starts with sk-proj- or sk-).
    • Anthropic: console.anthropic.com/settings/keys (starts with sk-ant-).
    • Qwen: dashscope.console.aliyun.com/apiKey (starts with sk-).
    • DeepSeek: platform.deepseek.com/api_keys (starts with sk-).
    • Grok: console.x.ai → API keys (starts with xai-).
  3. Copy the key and paste it into the “API key (optional)” field after enabling “RAG Phenotype Prediction”.
  4. The key is stored only in your browser for that analysis; you will need to paste it again on a different device or after clearing browser data.

Cost: The provider bills you directly for the tokens used. A typical K-Sites run with ~25 unmeasured genes usually costs a few cents (USD), but this depends on the model, provider rates and abstract lengths. Check the provider's pricing page for current rates.

Severity Categories:
LETHALKnockout causes organism death
SEVEREMajor developmental or physiological defects
MODERATEDetectable but manageable phenotypes
MILDSubtle or conditional phenotypes
UNKNOWNInsufficient data for prediction
How RAG affects ranking:

RAG can change the safety level of an unmeasured gene, but it does not change the evidence tier or the composite score. Genes are sorted in this order:

  1. Safety level — SAFE → CAUTION → MODERATE_CONCERN → HIGH_CONCERN → AVOID.
  2. Evidence tier — direct knockout/deletion → experimental essentiality → ortholog / distant hint → estimate → predicted / none.
  3. Composite score — higher first, used only as a tie-breaker.

RAG produces a risk level that is converted to a safety level:

CRITICALUsually AVOID or HIGH_CONCERN
HIGHUsually MODERATE_CONCERN or HIGH_CONCERN
MEDIUMNo safety-level change by itself
LOWNo safety-level change by itself
UNKNOWNTreated exactly like no RAG prediction
Important rules:
  • Measured evidence overrides RAG. If a gene already has a direct knockout, essentiality screen, ortholog or estimate result, RAG is ignored for that gene.
  • RAG cannot improve rank. It can only raise the safety level (make a gene look riskier), never lower it.
  • UNKNOWN or LOW RAG gives no advantage. After a recent fix, a prediction of UNKNOWN or LOW is placed in the same evidence tier as no prediction at all, so enabling RAG does not boost unmeasured genes unless it returns a usable, non-UNKNOWN call.
Limitations:

It is only used for genes without a measured knockout result and without an estimate, and it is capped at 25 genes per job. We tested the free model on a random sample of 60 mouse genes with known outcomes: it recognised 7 of 30 lethal genes, wrongly called 4 of 30 viable genes lethal, and gave no answer for two thirds (see the Validation page). Treat any prediction as a hint to read the papers, not as a result.

Reference: RAG-based NLP pipeline using PubMed; optional hosted LLM fallback (Kimi, OpenAI, Anthropic, Qwen, DeepSeek, Grok)

Database Selection

Choose which biological databases contribute annotations to the pleiotropy score

QuickGO is mandatory and is always queried. You can additionally select any combination of:

  • UniProt — curated protein function and GO annotations
  • NCBI Entrez (Gene) — gene-specific GO annotations
  • Alliance of Genome Resources — integrated model-organism gene data
  • MyGene.info — aggregations of several gene-centric sources
  • Ensembl — gene annotation and orthology-linked GO terms
How it affects the result:

More databases usually find more Biological Process terms for a gene, which raises the pleiotropy score and can change which genes pass the max-pleiotropy filter. It can also improve the evidence quality score by adding experimentally supported annotations. Each extra source adds query time.

Tip: Selecting more databases increases the pleiotropy score and search quality but takes more time. For a first quick look, use the default; for a comprehensive search, select all sources.
Reference: QuickGO, UniProt, NCBI Entrez, Alliance of Genome Resources, MyGene.info, Ensembl

Potential Candidates

Unverified genes that still score well and may be worth testing

Next to the Gene Results and Summary tabs you may see a Potential Candidates button. It appears only when at least one unverified gene (no measured knockout, no estimate, and no usable RAG prediction) has a higher composite score than the best experimentally supported candidate in the main list.

What it shows:

Up to five genes with the highest composite scores among those whose evidence basis is none or predicted. These are not ranked above measured genes; they are simply highlighted as possible targets to consider if the top-ranked measured genes are unsuitable.

How to read it:
  • These genes have no direct knockout evidence in the database.
  • Their high composite score comes from specificity, annotation quality, literature support and conservation only.
  • They are suggestions for further reading, not validated safe knockouts.

If no unverified gene outscores the best supported candidate, the tab shows "No unverified genes outscored the best experimentally supported candidate".

Reference: Composite score tie-breaker for unmeasured genes

Cross-Species Conservation

In how many of five model species the gene, or an ortholog of it, exists

The Conservation Score is the share of five model species (human, mouse, rat, fruit fly and worm) that carry the gene or an ortholog of it. The organism you analyse counts for itself.

How it's calculated:
  • The orthologs come from the Alliance of Genome Resources. A call counts when at least three of its algorithms agree, or two agree and it is the best pair in both directions.
  • Score = species with the gene or an ortholog / 5, so it takes the values 0, 0.2, 0.4, 0.6, 0.8 and 1.
  • Orthologs are matched by identifier, not by name: the fly counterpart of PCNA is called mus209, and a gene with the same name in another species is not necessarily an ortholog.
Interpretation:
0-0.2Found in one species only: possibly species-specific
0.4-0.8Moderately conserved
1.0In all five species: an old, widely kept gene
Usage:

Genes kept in all five species are more often needed for basic functions, so the report notes it. It does not decide the verdict: the measured knockout result does. For organisms without orthology data (plants, for example) the conservation is searched for by gene name instead, which is less precise.

Reference: Alliance of Genome Resources orthologs (Validation page for the checks of the other scores)

Frequently Asked Questions

What is the difference between pleiotropy score and specificity score?

Pleiotropy score measures how many processes a gene is involved in (0=specific, high=broad). Specificity score is the inverse on a 0-1 scale (1 minus pleiotropy divided by 10), so higher values indicate more specific genes. Both convey the same information but in opposite directions.

How accurate is the phenotype prediction?

Not very, which is why it is only a fallback. On a random sample of 60 mouse genes with a known knockout outcome it recognised 7 of the 30 lethal genes, wrongly called 4 of the 30 viable genes lethal, and gave no answer for two thirds. Genes with a measured knockout result are never predicted. Always validate predictions experimentally.

How do I know the lethal / viable calls are right?

They are the published measurements, not guesses: the MGI literature curation and the IMPC knockout screen for mouse, SGD for yeast, ZFIN for zebrafish, FlyBase for fly, WormBase for worm, DepMap for human cell lines. We checked them against the original papers of 24 well-known knockouts in mouse, zebrafish, fly and worm (all 24 agree, see the Validation page), and the two mouse sources agree for 92% of the 7,500 genes that both cover. Each gene lists the papers (PubMed links) behind its result.

Why is a gene fine in human cells but marked AVOID in the mouse?

Because the two measure different things. A gene needed for embryonic development (such as TDG or POLB) kills the mouse embryo, yet cultured cells, which do not have to develop, often manage without it. Choose 'What will you knock out?' to make the verdict follow your system; both results are always shown.

What does 'not shown' mean?

Every gene of the GO term is assessed. The report shows the safest 'Genes to report' genes; the rest, and the genes above your pleiotropy cutoff, are listed under 'Genes assessed but not shown' with their measured knockout result, so a gene is never hidden without a trace.

Why does a gene say 'viable' but still carry a warning?

Viable knockouts can still have a shortened lifespan, be infertile, or show phenotypes in many organs; the report lists these. If some knockout lines of the gene were lethal (a different strain or allele) the gene is rated at least HIGH_CONCERN even when most lines were viable.

What if my gene has a high pleiotropy score?

High pleiotropy doesn't necessarily mean you shouldn't target the gene, but be prepared for complex phenotypes. Consider: 1) Using heterozygous or conditional knockouts, 2) Including pathway information in your interpretation, 3) Validating with an independent approach.

How long does an analysis take?

Typical runtime is 2-5 minutes for 5-10 genes using the default database. Factors affecting speed: number of genes, how many databases you selected (each extra source adds queries), phenotype prediction (adds ~1 min/gene), and database response times.

How do the database selection options work?

QuickGO is mandatory and always queried. You can toggle the other databases (UniProt, NCBI Entrez, Alliance of Genome Resources, MyGene.info, Ensembl) on or off. More databases can raise the pleiotropy score and improve annotation coverage, but each extra source makes the analysis slower. The disclaimer below the database cards reminds you of this trade-off.

What is the Potential Candidates tab?

It is a separate button next to 'Gene Results' and 'Summary'. It appears only when unverified genes (no measured knockout, no estimate, no usable RAG prediction) have a higher composite score than the best experimentally supported candidate. It highlights up to five such genes as possible targets for further reading, without changing the main ranking.

Why does enabling RAG with an API key not make the analysis faster?

The API key swaps the free browser keyword model for a hosted language model. Both still have to search PubMed, retrieve abstracts and process them. The hosted model is usually slower per gene but can give more reliable severity calls because it reads context. RAG itself is the slowest part of the run; the key changes accuracy, not speed.

Why is a gene ranked first even though it has no measured knockout data?

Genes are sorted first by safety level, then by evidence tier, then by composite score. An unverified gene with low pleiotropy can be marked SAFE (capped at CAUTION) and therefore appear above genes with measured lethal knockouts, which are placed in HIGH_CONCERN or AVOID. This is why the Potential Candidates tab and the evidence badges are shown: they separate 'looks safe on paper' from 'has been tested'.

What does 'Lookup failed' mean?

'Lookup failed' means one or more database queries could not be completed for that gene. Hover over or click the question-mark icon next to the message to see whether the failure was a network/retry issue or simply that no Biological Process annotations were found.
